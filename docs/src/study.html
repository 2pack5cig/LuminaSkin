Study guide · for the LuminaSkin team

Everything you need to explain LuminaSkin.

Each idea is explained the way you'd explain it to a friend: what it is, an everyday comparison, why we used it, and the honest catch. Read it once fully; then use the "Say it in one line" boxes and the judge questions at the end before you present.

Part A · The medical side

A1. Why skin AI fails on brown and dark skin

AI learns from examples. Most public skin photos come from Europe, the US and Australia, so most show light skin. On light skin, inflammation looks pink or red. On brown skin the same inflammation often looks purple, dark brown or grey, and healing often leaves darker or lighter marks. A model that learned "rash = red" simply doesn't recognise these.

Comparison: if you only ever saw photos of red apples, you might not recognise a green apple as an apple.

Say it in one line: "We trained and tested on Indian skin specifically, and we report dark-skin results separately."

A2. Skin-tone scales

  • Fitzpatrick (I–VI): types by how skin reacts to sun. I always burns, VI never burns and is darkest. Most Indian skin is III–V.
  • Monk Skin Tone (1–10): a 10-shade colour scale made by Google, designed to be more inclusive of darker tones.

A3. Triage vs diagnosis

Diagnosis = a doctor deciding exactly what it is. Triage = deciding how urgently someone needs care and pointing them the right way. LuminaSkin does triage: likely possibilities, urgent warnings, what not to do, and when to see a doctor.

Comparison: a hospital reception nurse sorting patients by urgency, not the surgeon.

A4. Steroid misuse and "tinea incognito"

Steroid creams calm inflammation and itch. That's useful for eczema, but on a fungal infection (ringworm) steroids weaken the skin's local defence, so the fungus spreads, and the rash changes its look so it's hard to recognise ("incognito" = in disguise). Cheap "combination" creams (steroid + antifungal + antibiotic) are sold over the counter in India. In a rural North India study (BMJ Open 2020), 80% of tinea patients who misused steroid creams had steroid-modified tinea. That's why LuminaSkin shows a "medicines to avoid" notice and asks "have you used a steroid cream?".

Careful wording: doctors do prescribe steroid creams for eczema and psoriasis. Our message is "not without a doctor's prescription", not "never".

These are fixed rules, not AI, so they work even when the AI is wrong or the photo is bad.

A6. Scabies: why photos aren't enough

Scabies mites and burrows are tiny, so photos often miss them. Doctors use the story: itching (often worse at night), other people at home itching too, and spots between fingers or on wrists. LuminaSkin's scabies screen uses these clues (international scabies criteria use itch + close contacts). If you live alone, both typical signs are needed instead.

A7. Moles and the ABCDE rule

Doctors look for warning signs in a mole: Asymmetry, Border uneven, Colours (several), Diameter over 6 mm, Evolution (changing). E is the strongest sign, which is why our Spot tracker measures change over time. In darker skin, the most common melanoma type appears on palms, soles and under nails, so any new or changing dark spot there is always flagged.

Part B · Data and fair testing

B1. Our datasets

  • SCIN (Google): phone photos + patients' own symptom answers, labelled by up to 3 dermatologists. Gives us the symptom data.
  • DermaCon-IN (IIT Hyderabad): clinic photos of South Indian patients, skin types III–VI, including children. Gave us 3,352 photos for our conditions, plus scabies and vitiligo.

B2. Train / validation / test

Training photos teach the model. Validation photos are used to choose settings (like a practice exam). Test photos are locked away and used once at the end (the real exam). If you keep checking the test set and tweaking, you're memorising the exam, and the score stops meaning anything.

B3. Data leakage

One patient may have 3 photos of the same rash. If one photo is in training and another in testing, the model can "recognise" the patient rather than the disease, and the score looks falsely high. So we split by patient: all of a patient's photos go to the same part.

Comparison: giving students the exact exam question in homework, then praising their exam score.

B4. Class imbalance

Some conditions have many photos (tinea ~1,000), others few (pityriasis rosea ~60). A lazy model could always guess the common ones and look "accurate". We give rare conditions more weight during training and report macro recall (below), which exposes laziness.

Part C · The AI, explained simply

C1. Foundation model and embeddings (Derm Foundation)

Google trained a large model on a huge number of skin images. We don't retrain it. We give it a photo and it returns 6,144 numbers, an embedding, which is a compact description of what the skin looks like (colour patterns, texture, shapes). Photos of similar rashes get similar numbers.

Comparison: an experienced dermatologist describing a photo in a very detailed checklist. We then only need to learn which checklist patterns mean which disease, and that needs far fewer examples. This is called transfer learning.

C2. Logistic regression (the head on top)

A simple model that learns how much each of the 6,144 numbers points towards each disease, then turns the total into probabilities that add up to 100%. Simple, fast, hard to overfit, and it does most of the work in LuminaSkin.

C3. EfficientNet-B0 (a small image model)

A compact convolutional neural network: it slides small "pattern detectors" over the photo, starting with edges and colours, then textures, then shapes. Pretrained on everyday images, then fine-tuned on our skin photos. It gives a second opinion and powers the heatmap.

C4. Gradient-boosted trees (the symptom model)

Many small decision trees ("itchy? → yes → on groin? → …"), each correcting the mistakes of the previous ones. Good with yes/no answers, and it copes when questions are skipped.

C5. Late fusion (combining the three)

Each model gives its own probabilities; a final step learns how much to trust each one and combines them. If the user skips the questions, a separate set of weights is used for photo-only mode. Combining helped: macro recall 60.7% (photo only) → 63.9% (with answers).

Comparison: asking three experts and weighting the one who's usually right more heavily.

C6. Grad-CAM++ heatmap

It traces back which parts of the photo pushed EfficientNet towards its answer, and colours them. Red = most influence. It shows where the model looked, not why, and it comes from EfficientNet only.

C7. Confidence and "Not confident"

A model can be 90% sure or 35% sure. Below a threshold (chosen on validation data), LuminaSkin says "Not confident, see a doctor" and hides its guesses. The trade-off: a stricter threshold means fewer but more reliable answers. Ours gives a confident answer ~82% of the time, and those are right (top-1) ~74% of the time.

C8. "Not skin / unusual photo" check

The AI only knows 15 conditions, so it must refuse things it was never meant to see. We compare the photo's embedding with the 10 most similar real rash photos from training. Too far → refused ("not a rash we can assess"); somewhat far → analysed but marked not confident. On 67 negative photos this cut confident wrong answers from 32 to 15.

Comparison: "Does this look like anything in my reference album? If not, I won't guess."

C9. Photo quality check

  • Blur: sharp photos have strong, sudden brightness changes at edges (measured with the "Laplacian"). Blurry ones don't. We measure at a fixed small size so low-resolution or smooth skin isn't wrongly called blurry.
  • Too dark: average brightness, set low enough that dark skin isn't mistaken for a dark photo.
  • Glare: only blown-out pure-white pixels count, so pale skin and vitiligo patches aren't mistaken for glare. It's a warning, not a block.

Why top-1 looks "low": 15 conditions, many look alike, and dermatologists themselves disagree on photos. Random guessing would get top-1 ≈ 7% and top-3 ≈ 20%.

Before vs after Indian data: on the same Indian test patients, top-3 went from 69.4% to 90.6%, and on Fitzpatrick V–VI from 71.2% to 94.2%. This is the strongest evidence for the project's purpose.

Part E · Safety and product decisions

  • Follow-up questions never change the AI's ranking. They only add hints ("your answers fit Impetigo") and "could also be" cards. This avoids confident wrong answers from untested rules.
  • Experiments we didn't ship: adding 277 web images (Fitzpatrick17k) didn't beat the baseline on held-out data, so we kept the simpler model. Measuring instead of assuming.
  • Privacy: photos aren't stored on the server; scan history and tracked spots stay on the phone; no login needed.
  • Calm design: emergencies are bold red; cautions are a softer tone, so users aren't frightened by everything.

Part F · The Spot tracker

F1. Measuring with a coin

A photo has pixels, not millimetres. A coin of known size gives the conversion: if a 25 mm coin is 500 pixels wide, each pixel is 0.05 mm. The app finds the coin (it detects circles), and the user taps the spot. Coin sizes changed between series (old ₹1 = 25 mm, new ₹1 = 20 mm), so the user picks coin + series or types the diameter.

F2. Outlining the spot

The app models the surrounding skin colour across the photo (so uneven lighting and shadows don't count as the spot), then keeps the area at least half as distinct as the spot's centre. Multi-coloured moles stay whole. Accuracy: within ~0.2–0.3 mm on test images, about 1 mm in real photos.

F3. Change and the concern bar

Each recheck compares size (in mm), colours and outline with last time. The concern bar (0–100) adds up transparent warning signs, weighting change most: Low = keep watching, Medium = get it checked, High = see a doctor within 2 weeks. Small changes under ~0.5 mm are treated as measurement noise. A "same spot?" check compares how similar the two photos look (calibrated: 5% false alarms).

Never say: "it detects cancer" or "it's safe". Say: "It measures change and flags warning signs so people get checked earlier."

Part G · Likely judge questions (with answers)

Is this a diagnosis?

No, it's triage: likely possibilities, urgent warnings and when to see a doctor. It hasn't been clinically validated.

How do you know it works on dark skin?

We added Indian clinic data and tested on held-out Indian patients: 90.8% top-3, and 94.2% on Fitzpatrick V–VI, up from 71.2% before. Top-3 is the same on dark and light skin (89.1% vs 89.4%). The caveat: only 8 Fitzpatrick VI test cases.

Why not just use ChatGPT on the photo?

General chat models aren't tested on dark skin, can confidently make things up, and give no measurable accuracy. Ours is measured on held-out data and has fixed safety rules.

What if the AI is wrong?

Several layers: photo checks, a not-skin check, a "not confident" result that hides guesses, red-flag rules independent of the AI, and always "see a doctor" guidance.

Why only 15 conditions?

Those have enough reliable data, especially on Indian skin. Others are handled by follow-up questions with "could also be" cards and "see a doctor".

Can it detect skin cancer?

No. The Spot tracker measures moles over time and flags warning signs (growth, new colours, uneven outline), the way doctors use the ABCDE rule, so people get checked sooner.

What happens to my photos?

They're analysed on our server and not stored; history stays on the phone.

What's the hardest technical problem you solved?

Fairness in the details: the photo-quality check was rejecting good dark-skin and vitiligo photos, and measuring moles in uneven light. We tuned both on real Indian photos.

What would you do next?

A clinical study with Indian dermatologists, more Fitzpatrick VI data, an "other condition" category, Hindi and regional languages, and an offline phone version.
